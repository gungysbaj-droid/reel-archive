#### 224. Capital in the Twenty-First Century

Cited as reference [1] in the essay ("Harvard University Press, 2014"), used for r > g. Matches the publisher record: Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, published 2014-04-15, translated by Arthur Goldhammer. Part and chapter titles below were checked against the contents page in the publisher's sample PDF; the summaries are original and not checked against the full text.

Creator: Thomas Piketty (trans. Arthur Goldhammer).

- [Harvard University Press](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674430006)
- [Publisher sample PDF (contents page)](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/file/feeds/PDF/9780674430006_sample.pdf)

**Notes** (Original part-level study notes, not a substitute for the book. Checked 2026-10-15. Limits: Part/chapter titles verified from publisher sample PDF contents page; summaries are original, from general knowledge, not checked against the full text. Not a full book text.)

- **Part One - Income and Capital (ch. 1-2)**: Sets out the basic concepts: national income, capital as the stock of wealth, the capital/income ratio and the capital share of income. Chapter 1 surveys output and income across countries, and chapter 2 argues that high growth (above 1-2% a year) is historically exceptional and driven by catch-up and population, so slow growth is the normal state and the future will look more like the past than the postwar boom.
- **Part Two - The Dynamics of the Capital/Income Ratio (ch. 3-6)**: Traces wealth over three centuries in Britain, France, Germany and the US. Capital stocks were about 6-7 years of national income before 1914, collapsed with the world wars, depression and taxation, and have been rebuilding. Piketty states his 'laws': capital share = r x (capital/income), and in the long run capital/income = savings rate / growth rate, so low growth pushes the ratio up. Chapter 6 discusses whether the capital-labor split is stable, with the elasticity of substitution as the key technical question.
- **Part Three - The Structure of Inequality (ch. 7-12)**: Chapters 7-8 compare how concentrated income and wealth are, using the 'two worlds' of Europe and the US. Chapter 9 explains the rise of very high labor income ('supermanagers') through pay-setting power, not marginal productivity. Chapter 10 shows wealth is far more concentrated than income. Chapter 11 argues inheritance's weight is returning towards nineteenth-century levels, with a 'rentier vs. supermanager' trade-off. Chapter 12 looks at global wealth inequality and the higher returns large fortunes earn. This is where the central inequality r > g does its work: when the return to capital exceeds growth, inherited wealth compounds faster than the economy and wages.
- **Part Four - Regulating Capital in the Twenty-First Century (ch. 13-16)**: Policy proposals: a modern 'social state' built on education, health and pensions; a more progressive income tax (with very high top rates); and the signature idea, a progressive annual global tax on capital with international transparency on bank data, as a realistic utopia. Chapter 16 treats public debt, favoring a capital tax or inflation over austerity to deal with it.

#### 225. Bullshit Jobs: A Theory

Cited as reference [2] ("Simon & Schuster, 2018"), used for the essay's hobby-vs-job and drudgery sections. Matches publisher and reference pages: Simon & Schuster, published May 2018, expanded from Graeber's 2013 essay 'On the Phenomenon of Bullshit Jobs'. Creator: David Graeber.

- [Simon & Schuster](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Bullshit-Jobs/David-Graeber/9781501143335)
- [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullshit_Jobs)

**Notes** (Original chapter-level study notes, not a substitute for the book. Checked 2026-10-15. Limits: Original summaries from general knowledge; the five-type taxonomy was cross-checked against Wikipedia. Chapter titles paraphrased, not verified against the contents page. Not full book text.)

- **1. What is a bullshit job?**: Defines the term: a job so pointless or pernicious that even the worker cannot justify its existence, yet who feels obliged to pretend otherwise. The test is the worker's own judgment, and Graeber distinguishes it from merely 'shit jobs' (badly paid, but useful).
- **2. What sorts of bullshit jobs are there?**: The five-part taxonomy: flunkies (make superiors feel important), goons (act aggressively for an employer, e.g. lobbyists, corporate lawyers), duct tapers (patch problems that should be fixed properly), box tickers (create the appearance that something is being done) and taskmasters (assign work to others who do not need supervision, or invent bullshit tasks).
- **3-4. Why do people in bullshit jobs report being unhappy? What is it like?**: Argues that the damage is psychological: loss of a sense of being a cause with an effect on the world, a kind of 'spiritual violence', and the burden of having to fake purpose. Draws on hundreds of testimonials.
- **5-6. Why are bullshit jobs proliferating? Why does society not object?**: Locates the cause in 'managerial feudalism': the finance-driven economy and the political logic of creating loyal retainers and status hierarchies. Society tolerates it because of a work ethic that treats suffering at work as moral worth, so people resent others who have meaningful work.
- **7. What are the political effects, and what can be done?**: Links bullshit jobs to resentment politics and argues for a universal basic income as one way out, to break the link between employment and the right to live.

- [Simon & Schuster / Zero Books (current edition)](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Capitalist-Realism/Mark-Fisher/9781803414300)
- [Google Books (Zero Books, 2009)](https://books.google.com/books/about/Capitalist_Realism.html?id=ibN3fGpW1DIC)
- [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalist_Realism)

**Notes** (Original thematic notes on a short essay-length book, not a substitute for it. Checked 2026-10-15. Limits: Original summaries from general knowledge plus Wikipedia; not checked against the text; section names are mine, not the book's chapter titles. Not full book text.)

- **Thesis: capitalist realism**: The sense that capitalism is the only viable political and economic system, and that it is impossible even to imagine a coherent alternative (the Jameson/Zizek line 'easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism'). It works as an atmosphere that conditions culture, work and education, not as an explicit ideology.
- **Reflexive impotence and business ontology**: Fisher's 'reflexive impotence': people know the system is broken but believe nothing can be done, so their knowing becomes part of the stasis. 'Business ontology' is the assumption that everything, including schools and hospitals, should be run as a business.
- **Mental health, bureaucracy and the state**: Argues the privatisation of stress: rising depression and anxiety are treated as individual chemical problems rather than social ones. Neoliberal 'market Stalinism' multiplies audits and targets, so bureaucracy is not removed but displaced into performance metrics. The state is used to defend corporate interests while public services are hollowed out.
- **Opening a gap**: The 2008 financial crisis cracked the facade. Fisher calls for making mental health a political issue, reviving the idea of a public sphere, and using small cracks to expose capitalism's inconsistencies and bring back a politics of the possible.
