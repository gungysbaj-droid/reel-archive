## 2. The author's own written treatment of the same subject

From "Fraud, Risk & Disputes", the fraud chapter of Sharma's Money & Ledger Systems specialization on Algoroq (free to read: https://algoroq.io/specializations/money-and-ledgers/fraud-and-disputes/). This is the closest thing to the video's likely substance that exists in verifiable text. His framing: fraud is the part of the money path where the adversary is smart and your ground truth shows up two months late.

**A risk decision is a service with an SLO (6.1).** The inline risk check sits on the checkout critical path with a hard latency budget (his worked example: 80 ms). A useful decision joins data the request does not carry - account age, device reputation, velocity counts, graph neighbourhood, prior disputes - fanned out across many stores. With 8 parallel stores each exceeding its p99 on 1% of calls, about 7.7% of decisions hit at least one tail, so the fan-out's p92 equals every store's p99. Two non-obvious rules follow. First, a feature that arrives late must be treated as missing, and the model must be trained for missingness: substituting a default (zero prior disputes, median account age) makes every request look like a clean established customer, so under a partial outage the system quietly scores everything lower-risk and you find out from chargebacks six weeks later. Second, "fail open" and "fail closed" are both wrong as blanket policies: failing closed turns a risk-service outage into a revenue outage, and failing open is exploitable because card testers watch decline rates in real time. The stronger option: the real deadline is fulfilment, not authorization. For physical goods, authorize with no risk decision, hold the capture, and run the full decision asynchronously before the warehouse picks the order. Map every product to its point of no return (shipment, key delivery, payout release); the synchronous decision only has to stop what is irreversible at authorization time.

**The decline threshold is a function of the order, not a property of the model (6.2).** Approving a fraudulent order costs the goods, (1 - m)·A for margin m and amount A, plus a fixed dispute cost F. Declining a good order costs at least the lost margin m·A. Break-even: decline when p(fraud) > mA / (A + F). Consequences teams rarely act on (his illustrative figures, with F = $20): a $100 retail order at 30% margin should be declined at a fraud probability of only 0.25, not 0.5; small orders are dominated by the fixed fee, so thresholds collapse at low amounts, exactly where card-testing concentrates; high-margin digital sellers should be MORE tolerant of risk, because a false decline costs them almost the whole ticket. In practice card-network monitoring programs (fraud/dispute ratio ceilings) override the per-order math for high-margin merchants, and customer lifetime value justifies a higher threshold for plausible repeat customers. There is also a third action between approve and decline: step-up authentication (3DS), which costs some abandoning good customers but shifts fraud-dispute liability to the issuer. That gives three bands - approve, challenge, decline - whose widths should be measured per issuer and device class, not set as one global cut-off.

**Rules and models both fail quietly, in different directions (6.3).** Rules express policy and respond to a live attack in minutes; models rank risk on smooth high-dimensional signals. Failure modes: in an engine with SQL-style three-valued logic, a rule like `ip_country != card_country AND amount > 200` silently stops firing when the geo-IP enrichment returns null (fails open, costs money for weeks); in JavaScript/Python semantics the same null makes the rule fire on everyone (fails closed, noticed in minutes). Rules rot through accumulation - every rule needs an owner, an expiry date, and a marginal-contribution report (fraud dollars caught only by this rule vs good dollars declined only by it), and removing rules one at a time because leave-one-out attribution undercounts redundancy. Models fail through calibration drift: the ranking holds while the meaning of the score moves, so recalibrate on mature labels on a schedule. Both leak through their edges: attackers probe hard thresholds ($499 against a $500 rule), and a volume spike just below any cut-off is itself a detection signal. And a rule engine is a deployment surface: analysts editing rules in a UI are shipping production code to the money path, so rules should go through diff, backtest against 90 days, shadow mode, and percentage rollout.

**Velocity and graph signals (6.4).** The strongest card-testing signal is usually decline velocity, not attempt velocity - testers generate far more issuer declines than real customers, and declines are cheap to count. Counters must be increment-then-compare and atomic, counting the in-flight request itself, or a burst of fifty parallel attempts all read zero and all pass. Storage is dominated by cardinality times window count (his estimate: about 60 GB of hot state for 30M cards + 20M devices + 10M IPs at minute/hour resolution). Hot keys (a popular BIN or shared IP under attack) get split into sub-counters summed on read. Graph signals catch what velocity misses: coordinated fraud rings that spread activity so no single entity looks hot. The structural failure mode is supernodes - carrier-grade NAT addresses, default device fingerprints, a freight forwarder's warehouse. Traverse one and a single chargeback taints tens of thousands of legitimate customers. Fixes: down-weight edges by inverse log degree, refuse to traverse nodes above a degree cap, compute components offline on a pruned graph, serve only one-hop lookups plus precomputed aggregates at request time (a live multi-hop traversal has unbounded latency), and make label propagation auditable so you can show a customer which edge blocked them.

**Chargebacks are a ledger workflow with a regulator attached (6.5).** Money moves at the start, not the end: the acquirer debits the amount plus fee from settlement around day 45, and the fee is typically not refunded even if you win. Model the open dispute as a contested receivable, never as a reversed sale (anti-pattern: revenue drops on day 45, rises on a win on day 90, and win rate becomes unreadable). Post from exactly one source, keyed by (dispute_id, transition) - the same chargeback arrives as a webhook, a settlement line, and a report row, and posting from two sources books it twice. Four timing facts: cardholders can dispute about 120 days out, so this month's dispute ratio is last quarter's fraud (forecast ratios from transaction-date cohorts, or a post-holiday volume drop can push you over a monitoring threshold with no change in fraud); your evidence deadline is the processor's, shorter than the network's, so evidence assembly must be an automated pipeline; refunds and disputes race (lock refunds on dispute receipt; pre-dispute alert services like Visa RDR and Mastercard Ethoca convert would-be chargebacks into refunds but do NOT remove the issuer's fraud report, which Visa's 2025 VAMP program counts in the same ratio); escalation to pre-arbitration/arbitration is an expected-value decision - representment is worth automating for almost everything (break-even about $43 at a 35% win rate, so the common "only fight above $100" rule gives away money), while escalation rarely breaks even below hundreds of dollars and its win rate must be measured conditional on the issuer having already rejected your evidence once. On liability: 3DS moves liability only for fraud-coded card-not-present disputes (it does nothing for "not received" or "not as described", which is where first-party fraud lives), and it moves the loss, not the fraud report - a merchant that pushes everything through 3DS can see fraud losses fall while its monitored fraud ratio does not. Visa's Compelling Evidence 3.0 (2023) is the one change that makes engineering data directly worth money: you can defeat certain fraud-coded disputes by showing at least two prior undisputed transactions on the same card, 120-365 days old, sharing at least two data elements with the disputed one, IP or device ID among them - but only if you retained and indexed that data per transaction long before the dispute arrived.

**Case study: card testing.** A tester generates tens of thousands of small authorizations; the attacker is gone before any fraud label exists, so nothing supervised can respond in time and defence is purely rate-based. His illustrative bill: 60,000 attempts x $0.10 per-auth = $6,000 in fees before one card is even misused, plus an issuer-reputation dip that lowers approvals for real customers. Auth-validation endpoints (card-on-file setup, $0 checks, free trials) deserve stricter budgets than checkout because they produce a validated card with no order to review. When a velocity rule trips, return the same response an issuer decline would produce, at the same latency - a distinct "blocked" error tells the tester which cards were never tested. And declare the incident on a decline-rate anomaly, not on fraud losses: by the time losses show up the attack is two months old.

## 3. What PayPal itself has documented about its real risk stack

Independent of the video and of Sharma: PayPal's own engineering blog ("How PayPal Uses Real-time Graph Database and Graph Analysis to Fight Fraud", PayPal Tech via Medium, Dec 2021, by Quinn Zuo, David Zhang, Yan Zhang, Nitin Sharma: https://medium.com/paypal-tech/how-paypal-uses-real-time-graph-database-and-graph-analysis-to-fight-fraud-96a2b918619a).

- Scale (2021 figures): 400M+ active consumers and merchants, several thousand payment transactions per second. The relationships in the payment network are strong predictors of fraud but too large for a relational database, so PayPal built a home-grown real-time graph database treating relationships as first-class citizens.
- Two analysis types: discrete graph analysis (risk-scoring vertices by association with negative lists and anomalies) and connected graph analysis (page rank, connected components, clustering, centrality to tell a friend group from a coordinated fraud ring).
- Three cooperating stacks: real-time (sub-second queries feeding risk-strategy rules and ML embeddings, for online fraud prevention), interactive (seconds to minutes, visualization, for risk back-office and AML investigations), and analytics (offline graph algorithms, feature and embedding creation, graph ML training).
- Requirements they set for the in-house real-time graph DB after rejecting vendors and open source: sub-second query latency, seconds-level data freshness, horizontal scalability with fault tolerance, million-QPS throughput, offline-to-online backfill, and compliance with PayPal's data security rules. Writes come from offline batch snapshot/delta loading plus event-based near-real-time updates (login, transaction and other business events create vertices and edges within seconds); reads go through a graph query engine with Gremlin (Apache open-source graph DSL) as the unified interface, embedded in the query service. A worked example: a two-hop query at the moment a merchant receives a payment, in about 10 ms, to catch repeat fraudsters.

The through-line with section 2: Sharma's chapter is the generalized engineering playbook; PayPal's blog is one company's production instantiation of the graph-signal layer of that playbook. Whether the video draws this connection is unknown (see provenance note).
